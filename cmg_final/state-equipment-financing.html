What Changes From State to State

State income tax. A business in a no-income-tax state such as Texas or Florida keeps more of its profit, which directly improves its ability to service equipment loan payments. The same gross income supports a larger equipment loan than it would in a high-tax state like California, New York, or Illinois.

Sales tax on purchases and leases. States tax equipment purchases and lease payments differently. Some states exempt or reduce sales tax on certain manufacturing or agricultural equipment, while others apply full sales tax to lease payments — which can meaningfully change whether a loan or a lease is the better structure.

Lender depth. States with strong regional banks (Frost Bank in Texas, for example) or large Farm Credit associations give borrowers more in-state options from lenders who understand local industries and collateral. Deeper local equipment resale markets also make lenders more willing to finance, because they recover better on repossessed collateral.

State programs. Capital Access Programs, SBA 504 loans through local Certified Development Companies, agricultural finance authorities, and manufacturing tax-abatement zones vary widely by state and can lower the effective cost of equipment or open doors for newer businesses.

Frequently Asked Questions — State Equipment Financing

Does equipment financing differ from state to state?

Yes. While federal benefits like the Section 179 deduction apply nationwide, the lender landscape, state income tax, sales-tax treatment of equipment purchases and leases, and state-specific loan and incentive programs all vary by state. A state with no income tax (such as Texas or Florida) leaves more cash flow to service equipment loans, and states with deep regional banking or Farm Credit networks often give borrowers better access to competitive equipment loan rates.

Which states have the best equipment financing markets?

Texas and California lead by total equipment financing volume due to their large oil & gas, construction, agriculture, and manufacturing economies. Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Ohio, Michigan, North Carolina, and New York all have deep, active lender markets. The best state for any individual borrower depends less on the state ranking and more on whether local lenders understand the specific equipment and industry involved.

Do I have to use a lender in my own state?

No. Most equipment finance companies lend nationwide, so a business in one state can finance equipment through a lender headquartered in another. That said, regional banks, Farm Credit associations, and state-chartered programs sometimes offer better terms to in-state borrowers because they understand local industries and collateral resale markets. The best approach is to compare both national lenders and in-state options.

Are there state programs that help finance equipment?

Many states run capital-access, loan-guarantee, or economic-development programs that improve equipment financing access — for example state Capital Access Programs (CAP), SBA 504 loans through local Certified Development Companies, agricultural finance authorities, and manufacturing tax-abatement zones. These programs can lower the effective cost of equipment or open lender doors for newer and smaller businesses that might not qualify for conventional credit alone.
