Overview

What Business Equipment Financing Covers

Business equipment financing is a form of asset-based lending that lets a company acquire the tools it needs to operate and grow without paying the full price up front. Instead of draining working capital on a large one-time purchase, you make predictable monthly payments while the equipment starts producing revenue on day one. The equipment itself secures the loan, which is why this is one of the most accessible forms of business credit — a lender can repossess and resell the asset if the loan defaults, so they take less risk than they would on an unsecured line of credit.

Almost any tangible, income-producing asset qualifies — heavy machinery, vehicles and trailers, production and fabrication equipment, medical and dental devices, commercial kitchen and restaurant equipment, warehouse and material-handling systems, technology, and specialty tools for nearly every trade. If a lender believes the asset holds resale value and helps your business make money, it can usually be financed.

Two structures dominate the market. An equipment loan (or a $1-buyout lease) is a path to ownership: you build equity in the asset and own it free and clear once the loan is paid off. An equipment lease — especially a fair-market-value lease — keeps monthly payments lower and lets you return or upgrade the equipment at the end of the term, which suits fast-depreciating or rapidly evolving technology. We compare both in detail below and in our dedicated equipment financing vs. lease guide.

Why finance instead of paying cash?

The strongest argument is preserving liquidity. Cash tied up in a depreciating machine cannot cover payroll, inventory, or an unexpected downturn. Financing keeps that cash in reserve and lets the equipment pay for itself out of the revenue it generates. It also builds business credit, and the interest is generally tax-deductible. Paired with a Section 179 write-off, first-year tax savings can exceed the payments you actually made — a rare case where borrowing improves cash flow rather than straining it.

How It Works

How Business Equipment Financing Works, Step by Step

1. Get an Equipment Quote

Choose the machine and get a written quote or invoice from the vendor. The purchase price, make/model, and whether it is new or used all shape your rate and term.

2. Apply & Pre-Qualify

Submit a short application. Requests under ~$150K are often application-only with a soft credit pull, so you can compare offers without dinging your score.

3. Underwriting

The lender reviews credit, time in business, bank statements, and the equipment as collateral. Well-documented deals are frequently approved within 24–48 hours.

4. Review Terms

Compare APR, term length, down payment, and any end-of-term buyout. Confirm there is no prepayment penalty if you may pay off early.

5. Vendor Funding

Once you sign, the lender pays the vendor directly and files a UCC-1 lien on the equipment. You take delivery and put the asset to work.

6. Repay & Own

You make fixed monthly payments for the term. With a loan or $1-buyout lease, you own the equipment outright at the end; with an FMV lease, you can upgrade or return it.

Loan vs. Lease

Equipment Loan vs. Equipment Lease

The right structure depends on how long you will keep the equipment and how quickly it loses value or becomes obsolete. Here is how the two compare on the factors that matter most.

FactorEquipment Loan / $1-Buyout LeaseFair-Market-Value (FMV) Lease
OwnershipYou own the asset (build equity)Lessor owns; you can buy, return, or upgrade
Monthly paymentHigherLower
Down payment0%–20% typicalOften $0 down / first & last

Total cost over asset lifeUsually lowerUsually higher if you buy at end
Obsolescence riskYou bear itLessor bears it

Rule of thumb: finance to own when the equipment has a long, stable useful life (construction machinery, ovens, lifts). Lease when you value the lowest payment and the freedom to upgrade — common with computers, imaging, and diagnostic technology. Also weigh new vs. used equipment financing, since used assets cost less to finance but carry shorter terms and slightly higher rates.

Tax Strategy

Section 179 & Bonus Depreciation on Financed Equipment

The tax code rewards businesses that invest in equipment, and the benefit is especially powerful when you finance. Under Section 179, you can deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment in the year you place it in service — even if you only made a few monthly payments. For the 2025 tax year, the Section 179 deduction limit is $2,500,000, with a dollar-for-dollar phase-out that begins once total equipment purchases exceed $4,000,000. The equipment must be used more than 50% for business and placed in service during the tax year.

Bonus depreciation works alongside Section 179 to cover amounts above the annual cap. Together they can produce a first-year deduction that generates tax savings larger than the payments you made that year — effectively letting reduced taxes subsidize part of the equipment. Because the rules change and depend on your entity type and taxable income, always confirm any specific purchase with your CPA.

Requirements

What Lenders Require & Documents to Prepare

Documentation scales with the size of the request. Smaller deals move fast on minimal paperwork; larger deals require a fuller financial picture.

Application-Only (Under ~$150K)

Most lenders approve on a one-page application plus 3–6 months of business bank statements. A soft credit pull and basic business verification are usually enough for a same-day or next-day decision.

Full Documentation ($150K+)

Expect to provide 2–3 years of business tax returns, year-to-date profit-and-loss and balance sheet, personal tax returns, and a personal financial statement for owners with 20%+ stakes.

Equipment Quote or Invoice

A written vendor quote listing make, model, condition, and price. The lender funds the vendor directly and uses the equipment as collateral.

Time in Business

Two-plus years earns the best pricing. Startups can still qualify through application-only programs, larger down payments, and strong personal credit.

Credit Profile

A 575–600 personal FICO is the common floor; 680+ unlocks the lowest rates and $0-down programs. Business credit history helps but is not always required.

Down Payment or Collateral

Ranges from $0 for A-tier borrowers to 15–25% for challenged credit. A larger deposit lowers both your rate and monthly payment.

Fast Approval

How to Get Approved for Equipment Financing Fast

Speed comes from being prepared. Lenders reward clean, complete applications — many well-documented deals fund within 24 to 48 hours. Use these steps to move from quote to funded fast.

  • Have your vendor quote ready. A written invoice with the exact make, model, condition, and price lets underwriting start immediately.
  • Gather 3–6 months of business bank statements. For application-only deals under $150K, this plus a one-page application is often all you need.
  • Check your personal credit first. Know your FICO going in. If it is below 640, be ready to offer a larger down payment or strong bank statements to offset the risk.
  • Ask for a soft-pull pre-qualification. Compare offers without stacking hard inquiries; only accept the best one, which triggers the single hard pull at funding.
  • Right-size the term. Match the term to the equipment's useful life. Overreaching on term to shrink the payment can raise your rate and total cost.
  • Work with a broker for tough credit or large deals. A broker shops multiple lenders at once, which matters most for challenged credit, startups, and six-figure requests.

Common Questions

Business Equipment Financing — FAQ

What credit score do I need for business equipment financing?
Most equipment lenders approve borrowers starting around a 600 personal FICO, and some application-only programs go as low as 575–580 for smaller amounts. A score of 680 or higher unlocks the best pricing and lowest down payments. Below 600, expect to provide a larger down payment (15–25%), show strong business bank statements, or accept higher rates. Because the equipment itself serves as collateral, equipment financing is generally more accessible than an unsecured business loan at the same credit level.
What interest rates should I expect on an equipment loan?
Business equipment financing rates typically run from about 6% to over 20% APR depending on credit, time in business, equipment type, and loan size. Well-qualified borrowers (700+ credit, 2+ years in business, new equipment) often land in the 6–10% range. Mid-tier credit (640–699) generally sees 10–16%. Newer businesses or scores under 640 usually fall in the 16–22%+ range. New equipment prices lower than used, and larger loans ($100K+) usually earn better rates than small-ticket deals.
What loan terms are available for business equipment?
Equipment loan terms usually run 24 to 84 months. Lenders match the term to the useful life of the equipment: short-lived assets like computers or restaurant smallwares are often financed over 24–36 months, while long-lived assets like CNC machines, construction equipment, or medical imaging can stretch to 60–84 months. Longer terms lower the monthly payment but increase total interest paid, so choose the shortest term your cash flow comfortably supports.
How does Section 179 work with financed equipment?
Section 179 lets you deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment in the year you place it in service — even if you financed it and made only a few payments. For 2025 the deduction limit is $2,500,000 with a phase-out beginning at $4,000,000 in purchases. Bonus depreciation covers additional amounts above the Section 179 cap. Because you can write off the entire cost while spreading payments over years, financing plus Section 179 often means the first-year tax savings exceed the payments you actually made. Always confirm specifics with your CPA.
Should I choose an equipment loan or an equipment lease?
Choose a loan (or a $1-buyout lease) when you plan to keep the equipment long-term and want to own it outright and build equity — ideal for machinery with a long useful life. Choose a fair-market-value (FMV) lease when you want the lowest monthly payment, plan to upgrade frequently, or want to avoid obsolescence risk on fast-changing technology like computers or diagnostic equipment. Loans usually cost less over the full life of long-lived assets; leases preserve cash and flexibility.
What documents do lenders require for equipment financing?
For requests under about $150,000, many lenders offer application-only approval requiring just a one-page application and a few months of business bank statements. Above that threshold, lenders typically request 2–3 years of business tax returns, year-to-date profit-and-loss and balance sheets, personal tax returns, a personal financial statement, and an equipment quote or invoice from the vendor. Startups and larger deals may also need a business plan or projections.
Can a startup or new business finance equipment?
Yes. Startups under two years old can finance equipment, though options are narrower and pricing is higher. The most accessible paths are application-only programs under $75,000, deals where the borrower puts 10–20% down, and lenders that weigh strong personal credit (680+) and industry experience. Buying quality used equipment for the first year to build business credit and cash flow, then financing new equipment later, is a common startup strategy.
Does applying hurt my credit — soft or hard pull?
Most initial equipment financing pre-qualifications use a soft credit pull, which does not affect your score and lets you compare offers. A hard inquiry — which can temporarily lower your score a few points — usually happens only when you accept an offer and move to final underwriting and funding. Ask any lender or broker up front whether the first step is a soft or hard pull so you can shop rates without stacking multiple hard inquiries.
