Overview

Small Business Equipment Financing Explained

Small business equipment financing is a form of secured lending in which the machine, vehicle, or system you are purchasing serves as the collateral for the loan. Because the lender can repossess and resell the equipment if payments stop, the underwriting is fundamentally easier than an unsecured term loan or a line of credit. That single structural fact is why a small business with limited operating history or an average credit profile can often be approved for a $40,000 machine when it would struggle to get an unsecured loan of the same size.

The concept is straightforward. You choose the equipment and get a quote or invoice from a vendor. The finance company pays the vendor directly, you take delivery, and you repay the finance company in fixed monthly installments over a term matched to the useful life of the asset. When the loan is paid off, you own the equipment outright. Leases work similarly but end with a purchase option, a renewal, or a return rather than automatic ownership — more on that distinction below.

For a small business, the appeal is preserving cash. Rather than draining a bank account to pay for a $60,000 asset up front, you keep working capital available for payroll, materials, and marketing while the equipment generates revenue that covers its own monthly payment. A well-chosen piece of equipment is meant to pay for itself: the crew, the production capacity, or the service it enables should produce more than the monthly cost of financing it. This "the asset earns its keep" logic is the heart of every sound equipment financing decision.

Nearly any business-use asset can be financed this way — construction and shop machinery, commercial vehicles and trailers, restaurant and kitchen equipment, medical and dental systems, manufacturing and CNC equipment, warehouse racking and forklifts, salon and gym equipment, and technology infrastructure. If it has a serial number, a resale market, and a clear business purpose, a lender will usually finance it. Eligibility

Who Qualifies for Small Business Equipment Financing

Qualification rests on a short list of factors, and the equipment being collateral means the bar is lower than most owners expect. Lenders weigh the owner's personal credit, time in business, the health of the business bank account, the type of equipment, and the size of the request. You do not need to be perfect on all five — strength in one area routinely offsets weakness in another. A startup with a 720 credit score and a 15% down payment is as fundable as a five-year-old business with a 640 score and steady deposits.

Personal Credit

The single most important factor. A 620 score opens most application-only programs; 680+ unlocks the lowest rates and the highest application-only limits. Below 600, expect larger down payments and higher rates, but financing is still available through second-tier lenders.

Time in Business

Two-plus years is the sweet spot for the best terms. Businesses with 6–24 months qualify through many programs. Day-one startups qualify through dedicated startup programs when owner credit and down payment are strong.

Bank Statements

Lenders want to see that monthly deposits comfortably cover the proposed payment, with few overdrafts or negative days. Three recent months is the standard ask for application-only requests.

Equipment Type

Titled, essential, resaleable assets (trucks, trailers, CNC, standard shop and restaurant gear) are easiest. Highly specialized or soft-cost-heavy purchases require stronger credit because resale value is thinner.

Request Size

Smaller requests relative to your revenue and history are approved with the least friction. Larger requests trigger more documentation and closer scrutiny of financial capacity.

Industry

Most industries are financeable, but some (trucking, certain hospitality, and cash-heavy sectors) face tighter terms. A clean bank profile and reasonable down payment offset industry concerns.

Fast-Track Approvals

Application-Only Financing (Under ~$150K)

The reason equipment financing is so accessible to small businesses is a category of programs called application-only financing. Under an application-only program, you can be approved on the strength of a single-page credit application plus a soft or standard credit pull — with no tax returns, no profit-and-loss statement, and no balance sheet required. Some lenders also review three months of bank statements, but the paperwork burden is minimal compared to a conventional business loan.

Most lenders cap application-only approvals at approximately $150,000. The exact ceiling flexes with your credit and industry: a strong 700+ borrower might get application-only decisions up to $250,000, while a thinner file might be capped at $50,000–$75,000 before documents are requested. Above the application-only line, financing moves to full documentation — the lender asks for two years of business and personal tax returns, current interim financials, and sometimes a debt schedule, because the dollar amount at risk justifies a deeper look at the company's capacity to repay.

Structure

Equipment Loan vs. Equipment Lease

Small businesses can acquire equipment through either a loan (also called an equipment finance agreement or EFA) or a lease. Both spread the cost over time; the difference is what happens at the end and how ownership, taxes, and flexibility work along the way. Neither is universally better — the right choice depends on how long you will keep the equipment, your tax strategy, and whether you want to own or upgrade.

ConsiderationEquipment Loan / EFAEquipment Lease
OwnershipYou own it when paid offLender owns it; you buy, renew, or return
Down payment$0–20% depending on creditOften first/last payment only
Best forLong-life equipment you will keepFast-obsolescing gear (tech) you will upgrade
End of termClear title, no balance$1 buyout, fair-market buyout, or return
Section 179Full deduction (you own it)$1-buyout leases qualify; true leases deduct payments

As a rule of thumb: finance (loan) equipment you intend to run into the ground and own — trucks, machinery, ovens, forklifts. Lease equipment that becomes obsolete quickly or that you expect to swap out — certain technology and imaging systems. A $1 buyout lease behaves almost exactly like a loan for tax purposes. Loan Type Comparison

SBA 7(a) & 504 vs. Equipment Financing

Owners often ask whether they should use an SBA loan or dedicated equipment financing. Both can fund equipment, but they serve different situations. SBA 7(a) and 504 loans, partially guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration, offer lower rates and longer terms — but they require heavy documentation and take weeks to close. Dedicated equipment financing is faster, lighter on paperwork, and uses the equipment as its own collateral, but rates are market-based rather than subsidized.

Many small businesses use both across their lifecycle, financing routine equipment purchases while saving SBA capacity for expansion.

Tax Strategy

Section 179 & First-Year Deductions

Section 179 of the tax code is one of the biggest reasons financing equipment can be smarter than paying cash. It lets a small business deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment in the year it is placed in service — up to $2,500,000 for 2025 — instead of depreciating that cost slowly over five to seven years. Bonus depreciation can apply on top for spending above the Section 179 cap.

Here is the part that matters for a financed purchase: you can deduct the entire cost of the equipment even though you only paid a portion of it in cash that year through your loan or lease payments. Suppose you finance a $100,000 machine, put nothing down, and make $12,000 in payments during the first year. You may still be able to deduct the full $100,000 under Section 179, potentially saving more in taxes than you paid out in payments. In effect, the deduction can offset a large share of the financing cost, which is why so many businesses buy equipment in Q4 and finance it before year-end.

Qualifying property includes most tangible business equipment — machinery, vehicles over certain weight thresholds, computers, office furniture, and off-the-shelf software. The deduction begins to phase out once total equipment purchases exceed an annual spending cap, and it cannot exceed your taxable business income for the year. Rules change annually and depend on your situation, so confirm the current-year limits with your CPA. Documentation

Documents You Need to Apply

Having your paperwork ready before you apply is the difference between funding in a day and funding in a week. The list scales with the size of the request. For most small business purchases you will only need the application-only set.

Under $50,000

One-page credit application, driver's license, and a voided business check or bank details. Many lenders approve on this alone with a soft credit pull. Fastest possible path to funding.

$50,000 – $150,000

Application plus the last three months of business bank statements. Occasionally the most recent business tax return. Still application-only at most lenders, still same-day capable.

Over $150,000

Full documentation: two years of business and personal tax returns, current profit-and-loss statement, balance sheet, and often a debt schedule listing existing obligations.

Always Required

The equipment invoice or quote from your vendor (with make, model, year, and price), and proof of business identity — EIN, business license, or articles of organization.

For Startups

Personal financial statement, evidence of the down payment funds, and sometimes a business plan or industry experience summary to support the owner's ability to operate the equipment.

For Used Equipment

Bill of sale or dealer quote, equipment condition or inspection details, and hours/mileage. See our used equipment financing guide for how age and condition affect terms.

Approval Playbook

How to Get Approved Fast

Speed in equipment financing comes from preparation, not luck. Small businesses that fund within 24–48 hours almost always do the same handful of things right. Follow this sequence and you remove nearly every reason a lender would slow down or decline your file.

  • Nail down the equipment first. Get a firm written quote or invoice from your vendor with make, model, year, and price. Lenders fund a specific asset, not a vague plan.
  • Assemble the document set before applying. Driver's license, voided business check, and the last three months of bank statements ready to attach. Every hour you save the lender is an hour off your funding time.
  • Know your credit going in. Pull your score so there are no surprises. If you sit at a tier boundary (say 678), consider a small down payment to nudge into better pricing.
  • Keep your bank account clean. Avoid overdrafts and negative days in the weeks before applying. Lenders read your statements as a real-time proof of cash flow.
  • Match the term to the asset. Ask for a term that fits the equipment's useful life. A sensible structure signals a sophisticated borrower and underwrites more easily.
  • Apply to a matcher, not one bank. A single lender may decline you for reasons that have nothing to do with your business. Getting matched to lenders that fit your credit tier and industry raises approval odds and improves pricing.

Avoid These

Common Small Business Financing Mistakes

Financing a term too long

Stretching a 3-year-life asset over 6 years leaves you paying on equipment that no longer earns. Match the term to useful life so you stay ahead of depreciation.

Chasing the lowest payment blindly

A low monthly figure often hides a long term and a high total cost. Compare the total of payments and the effective rate, not just the monthly number.

Applying to many banks at once

Scattering hard credit pulls across lenders can ding your score and looks like distress. Use a single matched application to shop offers cleanly.

Ignoring Section 179 timing

Equipment must be placed in service by year-end to deduct it that year. Missing December closing can cost a full year of tax savings.

Overlooking soft costs

Delivery, installation, training, and warranties may or may not be financeable. Confirm what is included so you are not caught paying cash for extras.

Skipping the fine print

Watch for prepayment penalties, required end-of-lease buyouts, and insurance requirements. Know the total obligation before you sign.

Common Questions

Small Business Equipment Financing — FAQ

How do small businesses qualify for equipment financing?
Small businesses qualify for equipment financing primarily on three factors: personal credit of the owner (620+ opens most programs, 680+ unlocks the best rates), time in business, and the equipment itself acting as collateral. Because the machine secures the loan, underwriting is easier than an unsecured working-capital loan. Most established small businesses with two-plus years of operation and clean bank statements are approved routinely. Newer businesses and startups can still qualify with strong personal credit, a reasonable down payment, or by financing essential, resaleable equipment.
What are the time-in-business and revenue minimums for small business equipment financing?
Most lenders prefer at least two years in business and $100,000–$150,000 in annual revenue for standard application-only programs, but this is not a hard wall. Many equipment finance companies fund businesses with 6–12 months of operating history, and dedicated startup programs approve day-one businesses when the owner has 680+ personal credit. Revenue matters more for larger requests: for a $30,000 machine most lenders simply want to see that monthly deposits comfortably cover the payment, while a $250,000 request triggers a full financial review including tax returns and interim financials.
What is the difference between application-only and full-documentation equipment financing?
Application-only financing means you can be approved on a one-page credit application and a bank-statement or soft-credit check alone, with no tax returns or financial statements required. Most lenders cap application-only approvals at approximately $150,000 (some at $75,000–$250,000 depending on credit strength and industry). Above that threshold, financing becomes full-documentation, or full-doc, requiring two years of business and personal tax returns, a current profit-and-loss statement, a balance sheet, and often a debt schedule. Application-only is faster (often same-day) but may carry slightly higher rates than a fully documented package.
Do I have to sign a personal guarantee for small business equipment financing?
In the vast majority of small business equipment financing, yes. A personal guarantee makes the business owner personally responsible for the debt if the business cannot pay, and it is standard for closely held companies, LLCs, and sole proprietors. Personal guarantees are what allow lenders to approve small businesses that lack a long corporate credit history. Larger, well-established companies with strong standalone business credit and significant revenue can sometimes negotiate a limited guarantee or a corporate-only structure, but a new or small business should expect to sign a full personal guarantee.
Can a startup or new business get equipment financing?
Yes. Startups routinely finance equipment through programs built specifically for businesses under two years old. Approval usually rests on the owner's personal credit (680+ is ideal), a down payment of 10–20%, and the resale value of the equipment being financed. Titled, essential, resaleable assets such as trucks, trailers, CNC machines, and standard construction or restaurant equipment are easiest to finance for a startup. Highly specialized or custom equipment is harder because it has a thinner resale market. Many owners finance a used machine in year one to build business credit before stepping up to newer equipment.
Should a small business use SBA loans or equipment financing?
Equipment financing is usually faster and simpler for a single machine or a small fleet — approvals can come in 24–48 hours, the equipment is the collateral, and paperwork is light. SBA 7(a) and 504 loans offer lower rates and longer terms and are better suited to large projects that blend equipment with real estate, buildout, or working capital, but they require extensive documentation and take weeks to close. A common strategy is to use dedicated equipment financing for machinery purchases and reserve SBA capacity for real estate or major expansion. Many businesses use both over time.
How fast can a small business get equipment financing?
For application-only requests under $150,000, small businesses are frequently approved within a few hours and funded within 24–48 hours once the equipment invoice and documents are in. The fastest path is to have your driver's license, a voided business check, the last three months of business bank statements, and the equipment quote ready before you apply. Full-documentation deals over $150,000 and SBA loans take longer — several days to several weeks — because of the additional financial review and, for SBA, government processing.
How does Section 179 help a small business that finances equipment?
Section 179 lets a small business deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment in the year it is placed in service, up to $2,500,000 for 2025, rather than depreciating it over many years. The powerful part for financed purchases is that you can deduct the entire equipment cost even though you only paid a fraction of it in cash that year through your loan or lease payments. This can make the first-year tax savings larger than the payments made, effectively lowering the net cost of financing. Confirm eligibility and current-year limits with a CPA, since the deduction phases out above an annual equipment spending cap.
