Overview

How No Money Down Equipment Financing Actually Works

"No money down" and "$0 down" mean the same thing in equipment finance: the lender advances 100% of the equipment's price, you pay nothing at signing, and your first monthly payment is typically due 30 days after funding. It is a genuine, widely available structure — but it is important to understand why lenders offer it and what they expect in return, so you do not walk in expecting a rate that only exists for buyers who put money down.

Equipment financing is fundamentally different from an unsecured loan because the machine itself is the collateral. When you finance a $60,000 excavator or a $120,000 CNC machining center, the lender files a UCC-1 lien on that specific asset. If payments stop, the lender can repossess the equipment and sell it to recover the balance. That collateral is what makes zero down possible in the first place — the lender is betting on the resale value of the machine rather than on a cash cushion from you.

The catch is that with nothing down, the lender's exposure is highest at the very start of the loan, when the outstanding balance is largest and the equipment has already taken its steepest first-year depreciation. To offset that risk, lenders reserve true $0 down for the applicants and the equipment they trust most: established businesses, solid personal credit, and machinery that holds its value on the used market. Understanding that logic is the key to qualifying — and to knowing when a lender's "0% down" advertisement genuinely applies to your situation.

Zero down vs. zero interest — two very different things

It is worth clearing up a common confusion. No money down refers to the down payment (what you pay at signing). 0% APR or 0% interest refers to the cost of the loan itself. They are unrelated. You can get $0 down at a normal market rate, and manufacturer promotions occasionally offer 0% interest while still requiring a down payment. This page is about the down-payment side. The Trade-Off

What Zero Down Costs You: $0 Down vs. 10% Down

The honest reality is that no money down is a convenience you pay for. When you put nothing down, you finance a larger balance, and because the lender takes on more risk, the rate is often slightly higher too. Read the table both ways. The 10% down deal wins on total interest and monthly payment. The $0 down deal wins on cash preservation — you keep $7,500 working in your business from day one. Neither is "better" in the abstract; it depends entirely on whether that $7,500 earns more deployed into inventory, payroll, marketing, or another revenue-producing machine than it costs you in extra interest. A business that can turn working capital into a strong return often comes out ahead with zero down even after paying more interest. Read the Fine Print

True $0 Down vs. First-and-Last vs. Deferred

Not every "no money down" offer means literally zero dollars out of pocket at signing. Three structures get marketed under the same banner, and knowing the difference protects you from surprises at closing.

True $0 Down

Nothing is due at signing. The full equipment price is financed and your first payment arrives about 30 days after funding. This is the strictest qualification tier — it is what established borrowers with strong credit and resellable equipment earn.

First and Last Payment

You pay the first and final monthly payments at signing — on a $75,000 deal that might be roughly $3,000–$3,200 up front. Common on $1-buyout leases and equipment finance agreements. Far cheaper than 10% down, but not literally zero. Always ask which one a quote actually is.

Deferred / 90-Day

Take delivery now and make no payment for 30, 60, or 90 days. Can be combined with $0 down so nothing leaves your account for up to three months. Interest usually still accrues during the deferral and is added to the balance.

Seasonal / Skip-Payment

Payments are structured around your revenue cycle — heavier in busy months, skipped or reduced in slow ones. Common in agriculture, landscaping, and snow removal. Pairs well with zero down for seasonal operators managing cash flow.

Step Payment

Lower payments early in the term that step up over time, letting a newer machine ramp up revenue before the payment peaks. Sometimes offered alongside $0 down for growing businesses.

90 Days Same as Cash

On some smaller-ticket programs, pay the balance within 90 days to avoid finance charges, or roll into a standard term. Useful when a receivable or seasonal payment is expected soon.

Deferred and 90-day-no-payment programs are among the most valuable tools for a business buying revenue-producing equipment. They let you install the machine, put it to work, and start collecting on the jobs it makes possible before the first payment is due. Combined with $0 down, a business can take delivery of a machine and have real income from it in hand before any cash leaves the door — the closest thing to letting the equipment pay for itself.

Startups & New Businesses

Can a Startup Get No Money Down?

This is the hardest case for zero down, and it is where honesty matters most. A business under one year old has no operating history for a lender to underwrite, so the default expectation is a 10–20% down payment. That does not close the door — it just changes the path. Startups reach $0 down (or close to it) through a handful of specific routes:

Strong personal credit and a personal guarantee. When the business cannot carry the risk, the owner does. A 700+ personal FICO with a personal guarantee is the single most common way a new business earns zero down.

Highly resellable equipment. Lenders relax down-payment requirements when the collateral is easy to move on the used market. A new landscaper financing a major-brand mower or a startup contractor financing a late-model skid steer has a far better shot at $0 down than one buying niche custom equipment.

Application-only programs. Many lenders approve requests under roughly $25,000–$50,000 with just a one-page application and a credit pull — no tax returns or financial statements — and these programs frequently allow zero down for borrowers with good personal credit.

Build, then upgrade. A very common and sensible strategy: put a modest down payment on your first machine, make 6–12 months of on-time payments to establish business credit, then qualify for true $0 down on the second or third purchase. Decision Guide

When No Money Down Makes Sense — and When It Doesn't

Zero Down Is Smart When

Cash is your constraint

Preserve working capital

The equipment produces revenue immediately, your cash earns more deployed elsewhere (inventory, payroll, another machine), you are protecting a cushion for slow months, or a deferred-payment window lets the machine start paying before the first bill. Growth-stage businesses with more opportunities than cash usually favor zero down.

A Down Payment Is Smarter When

You have idle cash

Minimize total cost

You are sitting on cash with no higher-return use, you want the lowest possible rate and monthly payment, you are financing equipment that depreciates fast (staying ahead of the value curve), or your credit is borderline and a down payment is what turns a decline into an approval. Putting 10–20% down lowers both the balance and often the rate.

The Middle Path

Best of both

Small down, better rate

Many businesses put a modest 5–10% down rather than choosing an extreme. That is often enough to earn a lower rate and shrink the balance while still preserving most of your cash. Ask any lender to quote both $0 down and a small-down scenario so you can compare total cost side by side.

The single most important habit: compare deals on total cost of ownership, not the monthly payment alone. A lower payment can hide a longer term and more total interest. Model each option — see the financing calculator — and factor in Section 179 and bonus depreciation, which can let you deduct much of the equipment cost the same year you buy regardless of how little you put down.

Getting Approved

How to Qualify for $0 Down Equipment Financing

Know Your Credit

Pull your personal FICO before applying. Aim for 650–680+ for true $0 down and 700+ for the best rates. Clear up any errors, and avoid opening new personal debt in the weeks before you apply.

Have Documentation Ready

For deals under ~$50,000, expect an application-only process. Above that, have the last two years of business tax returns, recent bank statements, a P&L, and the equipment quote or invoice from the dealer ready to speed approval.

Choose Resellable Equipment

Zero down is easiest on titled, serialized, major-brand machinery with an active used market. If the equipment is niche or custom, expect a down-payment request and be ready to discuss additional collateral.

Expect a Personal Guarantee

Nearly every $0 down deal requires the owner to personally guarantee the loan. Be prepared for a UCC-1 lien on the equipment and a personal guarantee on the agreement — standard practice, not a red flag.

Ask the Right Questions

Confirm whether a quote is true $0 down or first-and-last, ask about deferred or seasonal options, and get the APR and total-of-payments in writing — not just the monthly figure.

Compare Multiple Offers

Rates and down-payment terms vary widely between lenders for the same borrower. Getting matched with several equipment-focused lenders at once is the fastest way to find the best zero-down structure for your situation.

Common Questions

No Money Down Equipment Financing — FAQ

Who qualifies for $0 down equipment financing?
No money down equipment financing is generally reserved for the strongest applicants: businesses with at least two years of operating history, a personal credit score around 650–680 or higher, and no recent bankruptcies, tax liens, or serious delinquencies. The equipment itself must be titled, serialized business machinery a lender can repossess and resell — items like construction equipment, trucks, CNC machines, and medical equipment. Newer or lightly used equipment from major brands qualifies more easily than very old or specialized machines, because the lender relies almost entirely on the collateral value when there is no cash down.
What credit score do I need for no money down equipment financing?
Most lenders want a personal FICO of roughly 650–680 or higher for true $0 down on standard business equipment, and 700+ gets the best combination of zero down and a competitive rate. Scores in the 600–650 range can sometimes still reach $0 down when the business has strong revenue, longer time in operation, or the equipment holds its value well. Below about 600, most lenders will ask for 10–20% down to offset the added risk, though some equipment-specific lenders still approve zero down at higher rates for established borrowers.
How does no money down affect my rate and monthly payment?
Putting nothing down means you finance 100% of the price plus any fees, so the loan balance is larger and the lender carries more risk from day one. That usually shows up as a slightly higher APR (often half a point to a couple of points above what a 10–20% down deal would earn) and a higher monthly payment because you are amortizing a bigger balance. Over a full 60-month term, the combination of the larger balance and the higher rate can add a meaningful amount to total interest. The trade is straightforward: you keep your cash now and pay somewhat more over time.
Is first and last payment the same as true $0 down?
No. A first-and-last-payment structure asks you to pay the first and final monthly payments at signing, which on a typical business equipment lease or loan might be a few thousand dollars up front. True $0 down means you pay nothing at signing and the first payment is due 30 days later. First-and-last is common on equipment finance agreements and $1-buyout leases and is far cheaper than a 10–20% down payment, but it is not literally zero out of pocket. Always confirm whether a quote is genuine $0 down or first-and-last before you sign.
What are deferred payment and 90-days-no-payment equipment programs?
Deferred and 90-day-no-payment programs let you take delivery of the equipment now and make no payment for 30, 60, or 90 days. They are especially common on seasonal equipment (agriculture, landscaping, snow removal) and let a business install the machine, put it to work, and generate revenue before the first payment is due. Interest still accrues during the deferral in most cases, so the deferred amount is added to the balance or spread across later payments. A deferred-payment deal can be combined with $0 down, giving a business up to three months of use before any cash leaves the door.
Can a startup get no money down equipment financing?
It is harder but not impossible. Pure startups under one year old usually need 10–20% down because they have no business track record for the lender to underwrite. The paths to $0 down for a new business are: strong personal credit (700+) with a personal guarantee, financing readily resellable equipment that holds value, using an application-only program under about $25,000–$50,000, or bringing an established co-signer. Many startups begin with a modest down payment on their first machine to build business credit, then qualify for true zero down on their second or third purchase.
With no down payment, is the equipment the only collateral?
In most $0 down equipment deals the financed machine is the primary collateral, secured by a UCC-1 lien, and the lender also requires a personal guarantee from the owner. Because there is no cash equity cushion, lenders lean heavily on how well the specific equipment retains resale value. High-demand items from major brands are easiest to finance with zero down. Highly specialized, custom, or rapidly depreciating equipment may require a down payment or additional collateral because the machine alone would not cover the balance if it had to be repossessed early.
Is no money down equipment financing worth it?
It depends on what your cash can earn elsewhere. Zero down is worth it when the equipment will generate revenue immediately and your working capital is better used on inventory, payroll, or growth than on a down payment. It is less attractive when you have idle cash and want to minimize total interest, since a 10–20% down payment lowers both the balance and often the rate. Many businesses split the difference and put a small amount down to secure a better rate while preserving most of their cash. Run both scenarios on total cost, not just the monthly payment, before deciding.
